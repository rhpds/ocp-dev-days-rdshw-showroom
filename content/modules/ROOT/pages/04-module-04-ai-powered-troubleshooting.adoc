= Module 4: AI-powered troubleshooting
include::partial$_attributes.adoc[]
:imagesdir: ../assets/images

Your team at Parasol Insurance has been experimenting with a couple of R&D projects on OpenShift -- a web frontend/backend prototype and a new payments processing service. These are not in production yet, but if the testing goes well, they will be. A colleague mentions that the web prototype is timing out, and you have noticed some alerts firing on the payments app. Time to figure out what is going on before the next demo with stakeholders.

Fortunately, OpenShift includes built-in AI capabilities that help developers investigate application problems without needing to memorize `oc` commands, write PromQL queries, or navigate between multiple console views. OpenShift Lightspeed uses the Model Context Protocol (MCP) to query your cluster in real time -- examining pod state, logs, metrics, alerts, and network policies -- and presents findings in plain language.

In this module, you will use these platform features to investigate and resolve issues in your R&D applications, and build custom monitoring dashboards to keep an eye on things as development progresses.

== Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

* Use OpenShift Lightspeed to investigate application issues through natural language
* Diagnose network connectivity problems using AI-driven cluster introspection
* Investigate firing alerts and identify root causes across correlated metrics and logs
* Request AI-assisted remediation actions (like rolling back a broken deployment)
* Generate interactive Perses charts and build custom monitoring dashboards

== Exercise 1: Investigate a timeout issue
*Estimated time: 10 minutes*

Let's start with something straightforward. The Parasol web prototype has a frontend that continuously attempts to connect to a backend service. Something is preventing the connection, and the frontend logs are full of timeout errors. This is the kind of issue that would normally have you digging through service definitions, network policies, and pod labels -- but instead, you will describe the symptom and let Lightspeed track it down.

=== Step 1: Check the symptoms

. In the OpenShift console, navigate to *Workloads > Pods* and select the `{user}-parasol-web` project.

. You should see two pods: `frontend` and `backend`. Both are running, but the frontend is not able to reach the backend.

. Click on the `frontend` pod, then select the *Logs* tab. You should see repeated timeout errors:
+
----
Trying to connect to backend-service...
ERROR: Connection timeout to backend-service!
----

=== Step 2: Ask Lightspeed to investigate

. Open the OpenShift Lightspeed chat by clicking the chat icon in the bottom-right corner of the console. Select the *troubleshooting* mode from the mode selector dropdown.
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-mode-selector.png[Lightspeed troubleshooting mode selector,link=self,window=blank,width=500]

. Ask Lightspeed to investigate the issue:
+
[source,role="copypaste"]
----
Investigate the timeout connection issue in the {user}-parasol-web namespace.
----
+
Lightspeed will query your cluster via MCP -- examining pods, services, network policies, and logs -- to identify the root cause.
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-mitigation-plan.png[Lightspeed investigation results,link=_images/agentic-troubleshooting-mitigation-plan.png,window=_blank]

. Review the findings. Lightspeed should identify that a *NetworkPolicy* named `backend-network-policy` is blocking traffic from the frontend to the backend. The policy only allows ingress from pods with the label `tier: backend`, but the frontend pod has the label `tier: frontend`.

=== Step 3: Fix the issue

Lightspeed identified the root cause -- a *NetworkPolicy* that was configured too restrictively, blocking the frontend from reaching the backend. Now ask Lightspeed to fix it:

[source,role="copypaste"]
----
Please fix the NetworkPolicy so that the frontend can reach the backend service.
----

NOTE: This is a mutable operation -- Lightspeed will modify a Kubernetes resource in your namespace. For this workshop, we have configured Lightspeed to allow read and write operations within your own namespaces (not across the cluster). Your organization's policies may require different configuration -- for example, restricting write operations entirely, or requiring explicit confirmation before changes are applied. This is the power of the platform: allowing your admins and platform engineers to align AI capabilities to your security and compliance policies.

Lightspeed will update the NetworkPolicy to allow traffic from the frontend pods. After the fix is applied, navigate back to the `frontend` pod and select the *Logs* tab. You should now see successful connections:

----
Trying to connect to backend-service...
Success!
----

TIP: If Lightspeed suggests the fix but does not apply it, you can ask _"Go ahead and apply that fix"_ or _"Please update the NetworkPolicy with the corrected configuration."_

That was a single-cause issue with a clear symptom. Lightspeed found the misconfigured NetworkPolicy, explained the problem, and fixed it -- all through conversation. But real-world problems are rarely that clean. In the next exercise, you will tackle something messier -- a cascading failure where one bad deployment triggers a chain reaction across multiple services.

== Exercise 2: Diagnose and fix a critical alert
*Estimated time: 15 minutes*

While you were investigating the frontend timeout, something more serious has surfaced. The Parasol payments application -- the one your team is preparing for its first stakeholder demo -- has alerts firing. The `PaymentErrorRateHigh` alert indicates that more than 15% of payment API requests are returning 5xx errors. But here is the thing: nobody touched the payments-api code. The root cause is somewhere else, buried in a chain of cascading failures across services. This is exactly the kind of problem where Lightspeed shines -- tracing the causal trail from symptom to source.

=== Step 1: Confirm the alert

. Navigate to *Observe > Alerting* in the OpenShift console. Select your `{user}-parasol-payments` namespace from the project dropdown at the top. You should see the `PaymentErrorRateHigh` alert (Critical) and may also see a `PostgresqlTooManyConnections` alert (Warning).
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-observe-alerting.png[Observe Alerting showing payment alerts,link=self,window=blank,width=700]
+
NOTE: If the alerts are not yet firing, wait a few minutes. A recent deployment may take a few minutes to start causing issues.

=== Step 2: Ask Lightspeed to investigate

. In the Lightspeed chat, ask it to investigate the alert:
+
[source,role="copypaste"]
----
Investigate the root cause of the PaymentErrorRateHigh alert in the {user}-parasol-payments namespace
----
+
Lightspeed will perform a multi-step investigation:
+
* Query the firing alerts and their thresholds
* Examine pod logs for the payments-api, reporting-service, and postgres
* Query Prometheus metrics for error rates and database connection counts
* Correlate the findings to identify the root cause
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-alert-investigation.png[Lightspeed alert investigation,link=_images/agentic-troubleshooting-alert-investigation.png,window=_blank]

. Review the findings. Lightspeed should identify the database connection exhaustion as the cause of the payment failures. If it suggests increasing `max_connections` or other database tuning, ask a follow-up to dig deeper into _why_ the connections are being exhausted:
+
[source,role="copypaste"]
----
Did anything change recently with the deployments in this namespace that could have caused this?
----
+
This should lead Lightspeed to discover that the `reporting-service` was recently updated to version `v1.0.2`, and that this version is the source of the connection leak. The full cascade looks like this:
+
.. The `reporting-service` was recently updated to version `v1.0.2`
.. This version has a bug that *leaks database connections* -- it opens connections but never closes them
.. The leaked connections exhaust the PostgreSQL `max_connections` limit (set to 20)
.. Once connections are exhausted, the `payments-api` can no longer connect to the database
.. Payment requests start failing with 5xx errors, triggering the `PaymentErrorRateHigh` alert

=== Step 3: Request a rollback

Now that you understand the root cause, ask Lightspeed to fix it:

[source,role="copypaste"]
----
Please roll back the reporting-service deployment to the previous version to fix this issue
----

Lightspeed will execute the rollback by patching the deployment image back to `v1.0.1` (the last known good version). You can watch the rollout in the OpenShift console under *Workloads > Deployments* in the `{user}-parasol-payments` namespace.

After the rollback:

* The reporting-service restarts with the working version
* Database connections are released
* The payments-api recovers and starts processing payments successfully
* The `PaymentErrorRateHigh` and `PostgresqlTooManyConnections` alerts resolve (this may take 1-2 minutes as Prometheus re-evaluates the alert conditions)

TIP: You can verify the fix by asking _"Is the PaymentErrorRateHigh alert still firing?"_ or _"Show me the current error rate for the payments API."_ If the alerts are still showing, give it another minute and check again.

== Exercise 3: Build a custom monitoring dashboard
*Estimated time: 10 minutes*

Now that the immediate fires are out, you want to set up ongoing monitoring for your applications. OpenShift Lightspeed can generate interactive Perses charts directly in the chat -- and you can export them to persistent dashboards that continue to show live metrics.

=== Step 1: Create a dashboard

. Navigate to *Observe > Dashboards (Perses)* in the OpenShift console.

. Click *Create* to create a new dashboard. Name it `Parasol Payments Monitor` and select your `{user}-parasol-payments` namespace.
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-create-dashboard.png[Creating a Perses dashboard,link=_images/agentic-troubleshooting-create-dashboard.png,window=_blank]

=== Step 2: Generate charts with Lightspeed

In the Lightspeed chat, you will generate three charts -- one at a time -- to build up a useful monitoring view for the payments application.

. First, ask for the HTTP request rate:
+
[source,role="copypaste",subs="attributes"]
----
Show me the HTTP request rate by response code for the payments-api service in the {user}-parasol-payments namespace over the last 30 minutes. Visualize it.
----
+
Lightspeed will generate an interactive Perses chart directly in the chat UI.
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-perses-inline.png[Inline Perses chart in Lightspeed chat,link=self,window=blank,width=700]

. Next, ask for the payment error rate:
+
[source,role="copypaste",subs="attributes"]
----
Show me the payment API error rate (5xx responses as a percentage of total requests) in the {user}-parasol-payments namespace over the last 30 minutes. Visualize it.
----

. Finally, ask for CPU usage:
+
[source,role="copypaste",subs="attributes"]
----
Show me the CPU usage by pod in the {user}-parasol-payments namespace over the last 30 minutes. Visualize it.
----

=== Step 3: Export to your dashboard

Now scroll back through the conversation and add the charts you find most useful to your dashboard.

. Click the *Add To Dashboard* button on a chart you want to keep. If you do not see the *Add To Dashboard* button, navigate to *Observe > Dashboards (Perses)* first and make sure you are viewing your `Parasol Payments Monitor` dashboard, then try again from the Lightspeed chat.
+
image::agentic-troubleshooting-add-to-dashboard.png[Add To Dashboard button,link=self,window=blank,width=700]

. The chart will be added to your dashboard. Repeat for each chart you want to keep.

. Once you have added the charts you want, minimize the Lightspeed chat by clicking the *_* button to reveal the full dashboard view, then click *Save* to persist it.

. Your charts are now saved and show live, updating metrics -- a custom monitoring dashboard built entirely through conversation.

This workflow allows you to build monitoring dashboards through natural language -- no need to hand-craft PromQL queries or manually configure chart panels. As you investigate issues, you can capture the most useful metrics into dashboards that persist beyond your conversation and serve as ongoing monitors for your applications.

== Key takeaways

* OpenShift Lightspeed provides AI-driven root cause analysis through natural language conversation
* The AI correlates Prometheus metrics, pod logs, events, and Kubernetes resource state to identify issues
* Developers can investigate and resolve issues in their own namespaces without cluster-admin access
* AI-assisted remediation (like rollbacks) can be requested through conversation
* Inline Perses charts can be generated during investigation and exported to persistent dashboards
* Custom dashboards built through natural language provide ongoing monitoring without PromQL expertise
